# Interview content

Nine sections, asked in this order. Each has a purpose (what planning uses it for), questions to cover (batch them: about 2–4 exchanges per section, skills more), and a completion criterion. Examples span fields on purpose — the person may be a nurse, an accountant, a founder, a student, a developer. Take the field from section 1 and phrase everything after it in that field's terms.

After a section, write into `avatar.md`: bullets in the user's words, then one line `*Planning read (hypothesis):* …`.

## 1. Who I am and what I do

**Purpose:** everything later is read through this. The first question of the whole interview is open and unprompted by any assumption about the field.

- **Ask first, verbatim in spirit:** "What do you do in life right now — work, study, something else?"
- What to call you, and what a normal week looks like in one or two sentences.
- Stage of life in one line, in their terms (first job, mid-career, between things, back to study…).

**Done when:** field (or "undecided", after one ask), current occupation(s), and a form of address are on the page.

## 2. Where I'm heading

**Purpose:** the yardstick for skills, priorities, and what gets cut.

- What should be true in 12 months that is not true now? Push to 1–3 outcomes, each checkable ("earn X", "pass Y", "ship Z", "move to W" — not "be better").
- The direction in 5 years, loose is fine: a role, a way of living, a question they want answered.
- What "enough" looks like — the point where more effort would not be worth it. Soft answers are recorded as given.

**Done when:** at least one outcome is stated so that a stranger could tell whether it happened — or the user says there is none yet, which is recorded as *undecided* — and "enough" was asked.

## 3. Skills

**Purpose:** the biggest section; a roadmap is only as honest as its skill picture. Self-assessment tracks real ability only loosely and improves when anchored to something done, so each skill gets a behaviour-based level and one piece of evidence.

**Level scale** (the five stages of the Dreyfus skill-acquisition model, in plain words). Craft and other skills:

| Level | Anchor |
|---|---|
| 1 | Follows instructions; can do it with the steps in front of them |
| 2 | Applies it to real cases alone, but stumbles on unusual ones |
| 3 | Plans and decides: handles unusual cases, chooses between approaches |
| 4 | Sees quickly what matters; others ask them |
| 5 | Others learn from them, or pay or trust them as an authority |

**Working skills** are about reliability, not expertise, so they use their own anchors:

| Level | Anchor |
|---|---|
| 1 | Rarely, even with a deadline or someone pushing |
| 2 | Only with an outside push: a deadline, a person, a rule |
| 3 | Usually on their own, in ordinary conditions |
| 4 | Reliably, also when tired, busy, or interrupted |
| 5 | Others rely on them for it, or learn it from them |

**Evidence** = a concrete instance (a stated pattern with no instance — "I answer emails the same day" — is not one): something done, finished, or shown, or an ongoing activity the person can point to by a specific case ("the March audit letter I answered"). "Nothing specific" is no evidence. Evidence must fit the claimed level — a named failure supports a **low** self-rating, so "three half-finished courses" is evidence for finishing at 1–2.

**The downgrade rule:** a claim of **3 or higher with no evidence is recorded one level lower**, and the user is told so plainly; the self-rated level goes in brackets: `2 (self: 3)`. Claims of 1–2 stand as given; the evidence cell then reads `no evidence` or the failure named. There is no level 0. A claim whose only evidence contradicts it (rates learning at 3, names the stalled course) counts as no evidence for that level; a range ("1 or 2") is recorded as its lower number.

**A skill that splits** (4 on client work, 2 on own projects) gets two rows, each with its own level and evidence.

**Procedure**

1. **Derive the list.** From section 1 and section 2, propose 5–8 craft skills this field and this goal require, from your own knowledge of the field. The user removes and adds; keep whatever remains, three or more. Do not use a stock list.
2. **Working skills — always offered, same five for everyone**, because they decide whether goals get finished (a skip still stands): starting things, finishing things, estimating how long things take, learning something new, holding focus (under interruption or tiredness — say the row's label in the way that fits the person's real problem, keeping the skill).
3. **Other skills** (communication, selling, leading, teaching…): ask "does your goal depend on any of these?" — it may share a message with the working skills — and add only what the user confirms.
4. **Per skill:** level by the matching anchors, then evidence. Two short questions each; batch a whole group in one message when the user answers fast. When the evidence answers come back as one paragraph, map them to skills and read back only the ambiguous mappings, one line each — a person who dislikes lists gets one sentence.
5. **Open question:** "What can you do that I wouldn't guess from all this?"
6. **Gaps:** "What do you think is missing for the goal?" Record it as their opinion. Measuring the distance from goal to skills belongs to the roadmap step, not here.

**Done when:** every listed skill has a level plus evidence (or `no evidence`), and the open and gap questions were asked.

## 4. Fixed commitments

**Purpose:** what the plan has to fit around.

- Work, study, care duties, recurring appointments: days and hours, confirmed (schedules in memory are often wrong).
- Deadlines in the next two months, hard ones only (exams, filings, launches, payments).

**Done when:** the recurring weekly shape and the deadlines are listed (undated ones marked `(date unconfirmed)`).

## 5. Resources and limits

**Purpose:** the difference between a plan and a wish.

- **Time:** hours per week that are really free for own projects, and which days.
- **Energy:** best and worst hours of the day; any block that is dead time after a heavy commitment; how many focused hours a day are realistic.
- **Money:** only "is there slack, or is every spend tight?" — no amounts.

**Done when:** a number for weekly free hours and a statement on energy are on the page.

## 6. How I actually work

**Purpose:** projects drop. Goals fail at four places (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006 meta-analysis on goal striving): getting started, keeping going, letting go, and overextending. Ask about each with a real instance.

- **Starting:** what does not getting started look like for you? Last time?
- **Keeping going:** where does momentum usually die — week 2, the boring middle, after an interruption?
- **Letting go:** how do you drop things — deliberately, or by drifting until they are quietly dead?
- **Overextending:** when did you last take on too much, and what tipped it?
- For each: what has worked, even once.

**Done when:** each of the four has a concrete instance or an honest "never happens".

## 7. Unfinished business

**Purpose:** the load a roadmap has to account for before adding anything.

- Projects and obligations currently open, each with its state in one line.
- Things hanging over their head that are not started.
- Anything dropped before, with one line on why (no story).

**Done when:** the open list is written and each item has a state.

## 8. Non-negotiables

**Purpose:** what the plan must not eat.

- What stays every week regardless of projects: sleep, movement, people, rest, faith, family time — whichever is theirs. Taken as constraints, in their words, with which days and how long asked and the reasons left alone.

**Done when:** the list is written, or the user says there is none.

## 9. Working with me

**Purpose:** two or three lines; the general rules already live in `COLLABORATION.md`, so only what is specific to this person.

- How direct to be; what tone lands badly.
- Topics that stay off the table.
- Options and a recommendation, or an open question — which they prefer when planning.

**Done when:** two or three lines are written.
